01

Hey Sammy

tryheysammy.com ↗

Hey Sammy helps people find recurring activities near them, understand how to join in and keep showing up. The goal is to make trying new things and building friendships easier.

Technical challenge: turn scattered websites, calendars and social listings into trustworthy local recommendations, then make that inventory fast and useful in a mobile app.

1. Built a bounded AI extraction pipeline

I separated source discovery, evidence collection, model extraction and validation. Category-specific searches find the right sources; code handles recurrence, geography and deduplication. Database leases and job locks, budgets, bounded retries and publication checks keep the production pipeline resumable and controlled.

  1. 01
    DiscoverFind relevant sources by city and activity category.
  2. 02
    Collect evidenceRank sources, fetch pages and retain useful evidence.
  3. 03
    ExtractNormalize facts into structured activity records.
  4. 04
    ValidateCheck locations and duplicates; calculate recurring dates in code.
  5. 05
    Review & refreshReview candidates and refresh facts as sources age.

2. Reduced repeated work in the app’s feed

I used AI-assisted engineering to share concurrent lookups, fetch related data together, cache inventory by account, ZIP and preferences, and index the actual database queries. Delayed requests cannot overwrite a newer selection.

~37 → 11database requests for three activity rows in an instrumented client test

App-side changes were local at that validation checkpoint; the indexes were applied live. This measures requests, not native launch time.

3. Compared models and retrieval tools on the whole workflow

I evaluated DeepSeek, GLM and Qwen through OpenRouter, and tested Monid/TinyFish retrieval. I compared completed extraction, source grounding, valid structured output and total workflow cost. Exa was researched as an optional source-coverage fallback.

A cheaper Long Beach configuration reached $0.55 in production-equivalent cost versus a prior known cost above $1.35, but its grounding was weaker. I kept that quality tradeoff explicit instead of treating lower spend as proof of an equivalent replacement.

4. Used PostHog MCP in the measurement loop

In August 2026, I used PostHog MCP for analytics queries, funnels and trends on Hey Sammy. That gave me a way to inspect product behavior through connected tools and use the findings to guide the next improvement.

The durable collection system is a bounded production pipeline. Serper is a configured third-party capability; I did not build a custom MCP server or a self-training production swarm.

02

SideQuest

ohsidequest.com ↗

SideQuest turns ordinary days into small real-world adventures through quests people can do solo or with friends. Its content includes themed packs and city and campus editions, with progress and memories built around participation.

Technical challenge: deliver an expanding, image-heavy catalog without making every user download it all, preserve offline progress and private memories, and build the tools to understand and grow the product.

1. Reworked content delivery and reduced app size

I challenged the assumption that every user should download every city’s artwork. Versioned downloads and durable local caching let content load when selected and remain available offline. I moved 1,492 images out of the initial bundle while retaining featured onboarding artwork.

The image-encoding pass saved 42.6 MB across 33 opaque images. All 27 badges stayed unchanged, PNG masters were preserved, and lossy JPEG results were visually reviewed.

2. Made progress and photos survive retries

I built durable photo storage and a retry-safe completion flow. Stable media IDs and an outbox support recovery after interrupted uploads without creating duplicate completions or XP. Private storage and participant-scoped sharing keep Journal photos separate from shared quest activity.

3. Built the commercial and measurement layer

I built a React, Node and SQLite partnership CRM with contact provenance, deduplicated imports, pipeline stages and campaign drafts. I also refined PostHog funnels, built aggregate institutional reporting, and used AI-assisted video workflows for captions, UI overlays and repeatable edits.

The CRM is local; live sending and production event connections need further integration.

Hire Line Dancers

hirelinedancers.com ↗

Hire Line Dancers helps event planners find and contact line dance instructors for weddings, parties and company events. Instructors can apply, manage their profiles and respond to inquiries.

Technical challenge: connect discovery, private conversations, instructor approval, membership billing and dependable follow-up across organizer, instructor and admin workspaces.

1. Connected discovery to private inquiry workflows

I built the instructor directory, profiles and role-based workspaces, with authenticated inquiries and private conversations. Matching and fallback recommendations account for service area, event type, whether an instructor is accepting inquiries, and prior contact.

Stripe card setup happens before review, with no charge before instructor approval. Membership activation, signature-verified webhooks, a customer portal and failed-renewal recovery connect billing to the application lifecycle.

3. Made follow-up a durable workflow

I built queued notifications and delivery recovery, with response deadlines and fallback recommendations. A delivery or read receipt does not count as a reply, and the organizer chooses whether to contact another instructor.

The newer inquiry and optional SMS flow was locally validated; its production rollout and provider configuration were pending at that checkpoint.

1. Account research & outreach briefs

Give a rep the account history, a current reason to call and relevant Trunk customer proof in one sourced brief, with a call opener, LinkedIn angle and email draft to review.

Account domain→CRM history + current sources→Buying roles + relevant proof→Rep-reviewed brief
  • Pilot: 20 accounts with two reps. Start with public account domains and approved public customer proof; add CRM history as access becomes available.
  • Measure: research time, unsupported facts, rep usefulness, draft edits and cost per accepted brief.

Pattern studied: Clay’s internal account-research and outbound workflow. For Trunk, adapt the brief to active projects, operational needs and the right field-search or submittal-review proof.

2. Inbound & event-lead triage

Turn one form or conference list into deduplicated records, evidence-based qualification and a clear owner with a next step. Put uncertain records in review.

Lead or badge scan→Match + enrich→Qualify + route→Rep-reviewed next step
  • Pilot: 50 historical leads from one source, then a reviewed live cohort. Check existing owners and open opportunities before creating anything.
  • Measure: missed qualified leads, seller agreement, duplicate and ownership errors, time to the correct owner and meetings held after activation.

Pattern studied: Drew Bredvick’s first GTM agent at Vercel and Vercel’s lead-processing reference. The reference requires business-specific services and prompts; it is not a finished Trunk integration.

3. Customer champion move alerts

When a known champion changes employers, verify the move and new account fit, then give the account owner relationship context and a warm-introduction draft. Flag the relationship gap at the previous customer, too.

Known champion→Verified employer change→Fit + ownership check→AE-reviewed alert
  • Pilot: 100 known champions after relationship review, once an authorized contact list and monitoring access are available.
  • Measure: verified moves, accepted alerts, introductions, meetings, qualified opportunities and cost per accepted signal.

Pattern studied: Regency Supply’s enrichment and role-change workflow, a construction-adjacent example. Data coverage and results would need to be established for Trunk’s accounts.

For all three: observe strong reps, establish a manual baseline, keep evidence attached and review initial CRM writes and outreach. Use Trunk’s actual approved tools. The pilots above are proposals; the project measurements describe my own work at their stated checkpoints.

              05 / Delivery & ownership

              A useful first ten days. A measured 90 days.

              Proposed sequence
              Days 1–30

              Establish & launch

              Agree definitions and baseline. Launch the personalized-dashboard pilot and a complementary budget-readiness journey. Establish identity/event QA and initial source/influence views.

              Acceptance: sample opportunities trace to evidence; replay is idempotent; suppression and ownership tests pass.
              Days 31–60

              Connect & compare

              Implement signal → enrich → score → route with bounded research agents. Compare cohorts, learn from rep rejections and evaluate one vendor against actual gaps.

              Acceptance: each action has provenance, status and owner; totals reconcile; low confidence escalates; a negative result is still interpretable.
              Days 61–90

              Scale what holds up

              Scale only the supported motion. Use holdouts where feasible, improve buying-group coverage, and make the build/buy/keep decision with an operating runbook.

              Acceptance: evidence changes a real decision; failure recovery works; someone besides the builder can run the workflow.

              This is a delivery sequence, not a guaranteed revenue timeline. Enterprise outcomes may mature after day 90.

              The first ten working daysDiscovery → definitions → low-risk quick win → dry run
              Days 1–2
              Interview stakeholders; inspect won, lost and stalled journeys; define sales-accepted qualification and the business decision attribution should improve.
              Days 3–4
              Map HubSpot objects and ownership, campaign IDs, offline capture, source preservation and data gaps. Baseline quality.
              Days 5–6
              Agree sourced versus influenced rules, the event contract and a focused pilot.
              Days 7–8
              Ship a low-risk quick win such as event capture/routing or unassigned demo-request handling. Backfill only defensible records.
              Days 9–10
              Dry-run representative journeys. Deliver a scorecard, runbook, named owner and backup.
              A bounded agent loop with accountable ownersParallel research, deterministic validation and human promotion gates

              I’d use a small, coordinated agent swarm for approved public account, workflow and customer-proof research. Each worker returns the same schema with citations. Deterministic merging and evidence checks precede owner routing; rep feedback becomes a held-out evaluation before a workflow change is promoted.

              Loop: observe → validate → decide → act within bounds → measure → improve. This is a controlled operating loop, not autonomous self-training.

              Controls: duplicate/customer/open-opportunity checks, suppression, territories, freshness, action IDs, rate limits, bounded retries and logs. Put human gates on claims, uncertain identity, unusual personalization, permissions, budget and sensitive materials—not every lookup. Assign an owner, backup and incident path.

              The scorecard and discovery conversationQuestions for Kevin and Ryan before committing scope

              Outcomes: accepted opportunities per eligible account, qualified meetings held, pilot progression, new-logo versus expansion, complete spend/cost, stage aging and data coverage/freshness.

              Guardrails: complaints, opt-outs, wrong personalization, duplicate outreach, rep rejection, delivery quality and operating effort.

              • Which segments adopt best, and how does a project evaluation become an enterprise relationship?
              • Where do stages stall, and how are event participation and referrals currently recorded?
              • How trustworthy are source fields, account associations and cost data today?
              • Which stack, access, consent and vendor constraints shape the first pilot?
